# Reading pressing data

What follows is how to turn the fields Discogs returns into a judgement. Treat
the plant reputations as priors, not verdicts: every plant has good and bad runs,
and a release with hundreds of happy owners outweighs a plant's reputation.

## Pressing plants

**Consistently good** - flat records, quiet surfaces, tight quality control:

- Optimal Media (Röbel, Germany) - the European workhorse for careful labels
- Schallplattenfabrik Pallas (Diepholz, Germany) - audiophile-grade, often used
  for reissue series that advertise it on the sleeve
- Record Industry (Haarlem, Netherlands) - large, long-established, reliable
- Quality Record Pressings / QRP (Salina, US) - audiophile specialist
- RTI, Record Technology Inc. (Camarillo, US)
- Le Vinyliste (France), Deepgrooves (Netherlands) - small, careful

**Variable** - high volume, outcome depends on the run:

- GZ Media (Loděnice, Czech Republic) - by far the world's largest; presses
  everything from audiophile reissues to supermarket repress. Plenty of excellent
  GZ records exist. Lean on the owner ratings here rather than the plant name.
- MPO (France), Sony DADC (Austria), Memphis Record Pressing (US),
  Vantiva / formerly Technicolor (Mexico, US) - mixed reports

**Caution** - frequent reports of noise, off-centre holes or dish warps:

- Independent Record Pressing, some Precision Record Pressing runs
- Anything a release's own Discogs reviews complain about, whoever pressed it

No plant credit at all is common on budget reissues and tells you little on its
own, but combined with no cut credit it is a reliable sign of a cheap repress.

## Green flags

- **A named lacquer-cut credit.** Bernie Grundman, Chris Bellman, Miles Showell,
  Kevin Gray, Ryan Smith, Translab, The Exchange, Alchemy at AIR, Abbey Road.
  Someone cut this for vinyl on purpose.
- **"Half-speed master", "AAA", "all-analogue", "cut from the original tapes".**
  These are claims a label has to stand behind, and they usually correlate with
  the rest of the production being careful.
- **2 LPs for a single album.** Four sides for 45 minutes means wider groove
  spacing and short sides - less inner-groove distortion, more level on the disc.
  This is a real engineering decision, not packaging.
- **Gatefold with printed or poly-lined inner sleeves.** Stiffer package that
  survives shipping, and the record is not sliding against bare paper.
- **180 g.** It does not sound better - groove geometry decides that - but it is
  stiffer and far more warp-resistant in transit and storage. Worth paying for
  if the user keeps getting warped records.
- **A high Discogs rating over a large sample.** 4.5+ across several hundred
  ratings is strong evidence. 5.0 from four ratings is noise.

## Red flags

- **A loudness-war-era album reissued with no remaster or cut credit.** Most
  2000s rock catalogue falls here: the only master that exists is the heavily
  limited CD master, and pressing it to vinyl inherits every bit of that.
- **"Limited edition" as the only distinguishing claim.** Colour and numbering
  are marketing; they say nothing about the cut or the plant.
- **Picture discs.** The groove sits on a laminate over a printed core. They look
  good and sound consistently worse. Fine as an object, bad as a record.
- **Very low price for a double LP from a major label.** Something was cut, and
  it is usually the mastering budget.
- **No owner ratings at all.** Not damning - new releases start at zero - but it
  means nobody has reported back yet, so the purchase carries more risk.

## Colour and transparency

Black vinyl is black because of carbon black in the compound. It also improves
release from the stamper and bleeds off static, which is why black is generally
the quietest surface. Coloured, splatter and translucent records swap the carbon
for dye and are, on average, slightly noisier - though a well-made colour
pressing from a good plant beats a careless black one easily.

The practical consequence: a translucent record is not evidence of quality, and
a buyer who starts shopping for translucency will end up with worse records.
When someone has drawn that conclusion from a good-sounding colour pressing, the
useful correction is that they liked the *cut*, not the plastic.

## Warps, and who is actually at fault

Records leave the plant flat and warp afterwards. The causes, in rough order:
stored flat under the weight of a stack, left in a hot vehicle or a sunny room,
shipped in a box with room to flex, or squeezed into a tight shelf at an angle.

So when a record arrives warped, handling and storage are a better explanation
than the pressing plant. Be precise about which one, though, rather than reaching
for the most dramatic story. A long delivery window usually reflects an ordering
cycle, not a long or hot journey, and a record that crossed a border in a
temperature-controlled freight network is not at obvious risk. What a longer
supply chain actually adds is handling hops and shelf time - the copy sat
somewhere, possibly flat under a stack, before anyone picked it.

Season and climate matter more than distance. A record shipped through a summer
heatwave, or left in a parcel locker or on a doorstep in the sun, is at real
risk; the same route in October is not. If you are going to name a cause, name
one that fits the circumstances the user actually described.

## Retail and shipping notes

The listing wording tells you how many hands the record passes through. A shop
that holds its own stock and ships next day puts the record through fewer hops
than one that orders it from a distributor or a partner warehouse on demand -
wording like "ships from our supplier", "special order", or a delivery window of
one to two weeks. The cost of the longer route is handling and time on a shelf,
not usually distance, so do not dress it up as an epic journey. It is a mild
risk factor worth one sentence, not a reason to avoid a good pressing.

Sealed media is frequently excluded from a shop's general right of return, while
still carrying a warranty or a defect policy. That distinction matters to the
user: a warped or noisy record is a defect claim, not a change of mind. Buyers
who send it back as a return get refused; buyers who report it as defective
generally get a replacement. Worth saying whenever a verdict flags warp risk.

In the EU and UK, consumers have a statutory distance-selling right of
withdrawal, but sealed audio recordings are a named exception once the seal is
broken. In the US it is entirely down to the retailer. Keep this general unless
the user says where they are - do not invent a specific shop's policy.
